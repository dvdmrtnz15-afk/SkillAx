# Context model

Diagnose these nine dimensions before wording. Use only evidence in the request. Label guesses as inferred.

## 1. Relationship proximity

Types: stranger, referred connection, new internal contact, external contact, peer or close peer, cross-functional partner, direct report, manager or senior leader, mentor or mentee, customer or vendor, friend.

Ask:

- Have they communicated before?
- Is there existing trust?
- Was the connection introduced by someone credible?
- Is the relationship personal, professional, or both?
- Is one person requesting guidance?

Greater proximity permits brevity, shorthand, humor, and less setup. Lower proximity needs clearer framing, careful warmth, and fewer assumptions.

## 2. Power distance

Formal power: reporting line, level, decision rights, hiring or performance authority.

Informal power: expertise, tenure, reputation, executive access, network, control of needed information.

Ask:

- Who is likely to feel evaluated?
- Who has more to gain or lose?
- Who controls the next step?
- Could the message be forwarded?
- Could either party influence the other's career?

Calibrate to perceived power. Downward messages need psychological safety. Upward messages need clarity and respect. Mentoring needs warmth without condescension. Requests must not create unintended pressure.

## 3. Career significance

Routine, moderately consequential, career relevant, or highly career significant.

High-significance events: interview or job opportunity, promotion, performance review, executive exposure, formal conflict, sensitive feedback, organizational change, reputation-sensitive introduction.

Higher significance → more intentional wording, stronger structure, fewer jokes and slang, clearer next steps, language that survives forwarding.

## 4. Organizational culture

Traditional or modern. Hierarchical or flat. Relationship-driven or process-driven. Formal or conversational. Risk-tolerant or risk-averse. Field-oriented or corporate. Internal or customer-facing.

Distinguish company culture, local team culture, and the relationship microculture. A conservative company can still contain a direct, warm sales team.

## 5. Sender personality

Baseline voice, not a costume: reserved or expressive, direct or diplomatic, traditional or modern, concise or explanatory, energetic or measured, formal or conversational, humor-forward or serious, mentor-like or peer-like.

The situation decides how much of that baseline to show.

## 6. Recipient personality

Infer only from available cues: sentence length, greeting, formality, gratitude, directness, emotional language, emoji, scheduling flexibility, titles, amount of context.

Use probabilistic language. Do not diagnose from one email.

## 7. Communication channel

- Email — complete context, durable record, career-significant notes, structured next steps
- Teams or chat — fast coordination, short updates, existing relationships, low-friction questions
- Text — established permission, time-sensitive coordination, personal or field relationships
- Phone or video — nuance, mentoring, sensitive topics, complex alignment, relationship building

Adjust length, greeting, formatting, formality, and explicitness to the channel. Do not paste an email into chat or a chat into a forwardable executive thread.

## 8. Conversation history

- First interaction or not
- Who initiated
- Delay
- Prior commitments
- Recipient's prior tone
- Unresolved questions
- Previous emotional context
- Responsiveness patterns

History is a tone budget. Familiarity expands acceptable expression. No history narrows it. History should change the opening and the amount of explanation.

## 9. Intended outcome

One primary outcome. At most one or two secondary outcomes.

Examples: schedule, encourage, correct, secure a commitment, escalate, preserve authority, restore alignment, gather information, strengthen the relationship.

Judge the draft by the response it is built to produce, not by how impressive it sounds alone.
