# Worked examples

These are patterns, not copy-paste scripture. Change voice to match the actual sender.

## 1. Delay after a candidate thank-you

Event: hiring manager replies one day late to a thoughtful thank-you that asked for a short call.

Observed: recipient thanked the sender, asked for time, tone is professionally warm, complete sentences.

Inferred: recipient may read the sender as a gatekeeper.

Unknown: whether the sender has final hiring authority.

Primary outcome: get two time options.
Secondary: keep the candidate encouraged without a guarantee.

Tone: warm, professionally conversational, moderately direct, low stiffness, high boundary integrity.

Risks: warmth as intimacy; delay as dismissal; encouragement as a job promise.

Draft:

Hi Maya — thank you for writing. I flagged your note yesterday so I wouldn't lose it, and the day ran long.

I'd like a short conversation on the process and what you're optimizing for in the next role. Send one morning and one afternoon option that work tomorrow or Wednesday, and I'll confirm one.

Looking forward to it.
— Jordan

Why it works: specific delay context, no autobiography, easy action, encouragement without a verdict.

## 2. Upward scheduling ask

Event: director needs 20 minutes with a VP after a missed SLA, to agree a recovery plan.

Observed: internal, hierarchical culture, email, first note on this incident.

Primary outcome: VP picks a slot.
Secondary: look in control without dumping blame downward in a forwardable thread.

Tone: respectful, direct, low warmth theater, high clarity, forwardable.

Risks: hedging the ask; overexplaining; sounding panicked or political.

Draft:

Hi Alicia — I need 20 minutes to lock a recovery plan for the Atlas SLA miss. I can come with the timeline, owner, and two options.

If Thursday 9:00 or 3:30 ET works, I'll send the pre-read by Wednesday 4:00. If those miss, send one 20-minute window before Friday noon.

Thank you.
— Sam

Why it works: the VP is not asked to diagnose the problem in the email. The next step is a choice, not a project.

## 3. Downward correction in chat

Event: manager notices a peer-visible Slack habit — committing dates in public channels without checking capacity.

Observed: established relationship, modern team, chat channel, not a PIP.

Primary outcome: change the behavior.
Secondary: keep safety high enough that the person can reply.

Tone: direct, warm enough, no sarcasm, private if the original miss was public.

Risks: public correction; joke that lands as contempt; vagueness.

Draft:

Got a minute on the Atlas date in #delivery? Before we lock dates in a public channel, run them by capacity first so we don't set a promise the team can't keep.

If the Friday date is already real, say so and I'll back it. If not, let's edit the thread and put the revised date in after standup.

Why it works: names the behavior, explains the operational why, offers a clean repair path, does not attack character.
